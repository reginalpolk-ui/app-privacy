Get Clean Support

Get Clean is a free addiction-help finder: one front door for a person who is overdosing, a person who needs help, and the family who loves them. It carries the national helplines, a 50-state treatment directory, SAMHSA's published overdose steps, and plain-language explainers on treatment and Narcan. Get Clean is published by Reginal Polk Technologies, Inc.

In an emergency, call 911 first. Get Clean is an information and routing tool — it does not diagnose, and nothing in it is medical advice.

How the app works

  • Three doors, in order: "Someone is overdosing" (Call 911 first and biggest, then SAMHSA's steps, which the app can read aloud), "I need help," and "My loved one needs help" (the family playbook).
  • No account, no sign-up, nothing uploaded. The numbers you save, the state you pick, and your checklist progress live only on your device.
  • Every state is in the directory. Illinois and Tennessee carry their own verified state lines; every other state is badged "National routing" and connects you to SAMHSA's National Helpline (1-800-662-4357), whose specialists route you to treatment near you.
  • Calls and texts open in your own phone and messaging apps — the app itself makes no network calls.

Common questions

  • Does Get Clean cost anything? No. People in trouble pay $0, forever — there is no paywall, nothing is for sale inside the app, and listings are never pay-to-rank. Get Clean is funded institutionally, off the app: opioid-settlement programs, agency licensing, and treatment-network partnerships.
  • Do I need an account? No. There is no sign-up anywhere in Get Clean, and the app never asks for your name, email, or location — you pick your own state from the directory.
  • How do I delete what the app remembers? Open "My saved numbers" and use Erase — two taps remove everything the app stored on this device, permanently. Because nothing ever leaves the device, there is nothing on a server to delete. See the Privacy Policy.
  • Is this medical advice? No. Get Clean is an information and routing tool. The overdose steps reproduce SAMHSA's published guidance, attributed in the app. In an emergency, call 911 first.
  • Why does my state only show the SAMHSA number? Only Illinois and Tennessee state lines have been verified against the state authority's own page, so only those two are printed. Every other state routes through SAMHSA's national specialists, who connect you to state-funded programs where you live — the badge in the app says exactly that.
